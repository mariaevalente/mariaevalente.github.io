Predictive HVAC control

A controller that forecasts every zone 30 minutes ahead and, every 15 minutes, picks the least energy-intensive action that keeps the occupied zones comfortable.

The question

Fixed schedules run whether or not anyone needs them. Rule-based controllers react only after a room has drifted. I wanted to know how much a controller gains by looking ahead, and whether the gain holds across very different buildings.

How I tested it

Every controller ran on the same building, over the same simulated year of 35,040 fifteen-minute steps, with the same Lisbon weather, occupancy and constraints. Only the control logic changed. Beating the fixed schedule shows what closing the loop is worth. Beating the rule-based controller shows what prediction adds on top.

The ten buildings are six ASHRAE 90.1 prototypes, one of them deliberately degraded, and four digital twins calibrated on real buildings. They range from 3 to 31 zones, and from independent rooftop units to coupled VRF and DOAS systems. I built the whole pipeline: scenario generation in EnergyPlus, 294 forecasting models, the controller as an EnergyPlus plugin, and the comparison tooling.

A second generation

Analysing where the first version fell short showed two structural problems. The second version keeps the same forecasts and reworks how a decision is made. It evaluates a median of 6.4 times fewer candidate actions per decision, and finds a fully comfortable action in 79.5% of decisions instead of 57.7%.

Towards real buildings

Before moving to pilot sites, I built an evaluation harness for models trained on real data:

  • walk-forward validation;
  • a persistence baseline that every model has to beat, and 20 of 23 free-running models did;
  • a monotonicity test, so that more cooling can never predict a warmer room;
  • an excitation analysis. Only 5 of 17 devices had enough variety in their history to learn from, which made the case for a controlled excitation campaign.
